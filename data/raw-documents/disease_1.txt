# Vitiligo Patient Information

## What is vitiligo?

Vitiligo is a long-term skin condition that causes some areas of your skin to lose their normal color. Your skin gets its color from a pigment called melanin. Melanin is made by special skin cells called melanocytes.

In vitiligo, melanocytes are lost or stop making enough pigment. This can cause light or white patches to appear on the skin.

Vitiligo can affect people with any skin color. It can appear on the face, hands, arms, feet, knees, elbows, or other areas of the body. It can also affect the inside of the mouth and nose and the hair in affected areas.

Vitiligo is not contagious. You cannot catch it from another person by touching, hugging, kissing, sharing food, or living with them.

Vitiligo is not caused by dirt or poor hygiene. It is not an infection.

## What are the symptoms of vitiligo?

The main symptom of vitiligo is loss of skin color.

You may notice pale or white patches on your skin. The patches may start as lighter areas and later become white. They can have smooth or irregular edges.

Vitiligo commonly appears on:

* Face
* Hands
* Fingers
* Wrists
* Arms
* Feet
* Knees
* Elbows
* Around the eyes
* Around the mouth
* Inside the mouth or nose
* Armpits
* Groin or other skin folds

Vitiligo can also affect hair. Hair in an affected area may become white or turn gray earlier than usual.

Most vitiligo patches do not hurt. Some people may have mild itching, especially when new patches are appearing.

The amount of affected skin is different for every person. Some people have a few small patches, while others have larger areas.

## Can vitiligo spread?

Vitiligo can stay stable for a long time, or new white patches can appear over time.

Some people have only a few small areas. Other people may develop larger areas of skin with less or no pigment.

Doctors cannot always predict how much the condition will spread.

The pattern can also be different. Some people have patches on both sides of the body, such as both hands or both knees. Other people may have patches mainly on one side or in one area.

If you notice new patches or changes in your existing patches, it is a good idea to speak with a dermatologist.

## Why does vitiligo happen?

Doctors do not know one exact cause for every person with vitiligo.

In the most common type of vitiligo, the immune system may mistakenly attack melanocytes. These are the cells that normally make the pigment that gives your skin its color.

This is called an autoimmune process.

Family history may increase your chance of developing vitiligo. Some people with vitiligo also have other autoimmune conditions, including some thyroid conditions.

Having a family member with vitiligo does not mean you will definitely develop it.

Many people develop vitiligo without knowing what started it.

## Can stress or skin injury cause new patches?

In some people, certain events may be linked with new vitiligo patches.

These may include severe sunburn, cuts, burns, or other skin injuries. Repeated rubbing or friction may also play a role.

When vitiligo appears after skin injury, this is called the Koebner phenomenon or Koebner response.

Stressful events may also be associated with vitiligo changes in some people. However, stress is not the only cause of vitiligo, and you should not blame yourself for having the condition.

Not every person with vitiligo has a clear trigger.

## Is vitiligo caused by food or poor hygiene?

No.

Vitiligo is not caused by being dirty or by poor personal hygiene.

There is also no proven special food or diet that can cure vitiligo.

Eating a balanced diet is still important for your general health, but food should not replace medical treatment.

You should also be careful with supplements. Vitamins or herbal products should not be used instead of treatment recommended by your doctor.

If you want to take supplements, ask your healthcare professional first, especially if you already take medicines.

## How is vitiligo diagnosed?

A doctor or dermatologist can usually diagnose vitiligo by looking at your skin and asking about your symptoms and medical history.

Sometimes the doctor uses a special ultraviolet light called a Wood's lamp. This can make areas of lost pigment easier to see.

A doctor may also ask about:

* Family history of vitiligo
* Other autoimmune diseases in your family
* Previous skin injuries
* Sunburn
* How the patches have changed
* How the condition affects your daily life

Sometimes blood tests are used to check for other autoimmune conditions, such as thyroid problems.

A skin biopsy is not needed for every patient. It may be considered when the diagnosis is unclear.

A new white patch should be checked by a healthcare professional because other skin conditions can also cause loss of skin color.

## Can vitiligo be cured?

There is currently no treatment that guarantees a permanent complete cure for vitiligo.

However, several treatments can help bring some color back to the skin or make the patches less noticeable.

Treatment does not work the same way for everyone.

Your dermatologist may consider:

* How much of your skin is affected
* Where the patches are located
* Whether your vitiligo is still active
* Your age
* Your general health
* Your previous treatments
* What you prefer

Treatment often takes months, and some treatments may need to be continued or repeated.

Even when skin color returns, the color may fade again later.

## What creams can treat vitiligo?

Some patients use prescription creams or ointments.

Topical corticosteroids may help some color return and may sometimes help control the development of new patches.

However, using steroid creams for too long or using them incorrectly can cause side effects, including thinning of the skin.

Tacrolimus and pimecrolimus are other medicines that may be used for vitiligo in some patients, especially on areas such as the face and neck.

Ruxolitinib is a cream that may be used for some people with non-segmental vitiligo. Approved use and availability can be different in different countries.

You should not start these medicines without medical advice. Your doctor should tell you which medicine is suitable, where to use it, and how long to use it.

## What is light treatment for vitiligo?

Light treatment is called phototherapy.

It uses controlled ultraviolet light to help restore some skin color.

Narrowband UVB is one type of phototherapy used for vitiligo. Treatment usually requires repeated sessions.

Phototherapy may be considered when vitiligo affects larger areas or when creams are not enough.

Targeted light treatment, such as excimer treatment, may be used for some smaller areas.

Medical light treatment should be planned and monitored by a healthcare professional.

Do not use tanning beds or unregulated home UV lamps as a replacement for medical phototherapy.

## Can surgery treat vitiligo?

Surgery may be an option for some people with stable vitiligo.

One option is a skin graft. In this procedure, healthy pigmented skin is taken from another part of the body and used on an area affected by vitiligo.

Other procedures can transfer pigment-producing cells to areas that have lost color.

Surgery is not suitable for everyone.

It is usually considered when the vitiligo has been stable because active vitiligo may continue to create new patches.

A dermatologist or specialist should decide whether surgery is appropriate for you.

## What is depigmentation treatment?

Depigmentation is a different treatment approach.

Instead of trying to bring color back to white patches, the doctor removes the remaining pigment from normal skin. The goal is to make the overall skin color more even.

This treatment may be considered for some adults with very extensive vitiligo.

Depigmentation can be long-lasting or permanent.

Because it removes natural skin pigment, it also reduces the skin's natural protection from sunlight.

This treatment needs careful discussion with a dermatologist before it is considered.

## How should I protect my skin from the sun?

Sun protection is very important if you have vitiligo.

Areas of skin without normal pigment can burn more easily because there is less melanin to protect the skin from ultraviolet light.

You can protect your skin by:

* Using sunscreen with a high SPF when you are outdoors
* Reapplying sunscreen as needed
* Wearing protective clothing
* Wearing a hat
* Avoiding tanning beds
* Avoiding deliberate tanning
* Trying to prevent sunburn

Sun protection does not cure vitiligo. It helps protect your skin from sunburn and UV damage.

Sun exposure can also make the difference between normal skin and white patches more noticeable.

## Can I prevent vitiligo from getting worse?

There is no guaranteed way to stop vitiligo from getting worse.

However, protecting your skin may help reduce some problems associated with the condition.

Try to avoid unnecessary skin injuries, severe sunburn, and repeated rubbing or friction.

Protect your skin from strong sunlight and use sunscreen regularly.

Do not use unproven treatments or special diets as a replacement for medical care.

Regular medical follow-up can help your dermatologist monitor changes and decide whether treatment is needed.

## Can vitiligo affect my mental health?

Yes.

Vitiligo changes the appearance of the skin, and this can affect confidence and emotional wellbeing.

You may feel embarrassed, worried, sad, or uncomfortable when other people notice the patches.

These feelings are common and they are valid.

You can talk with your family, friends, dermatologist, counselor, psychologist, or a support group.

Some people use camouflage makeup to make patches less visible. Other people prefer not to cover them.

There is no right or wrong choice. The important thing is what makes you feel comfortable.

## Can vitiligo affect the eyes, ears, or mouth?

Yes.

Vitiligo can cause loss of pigment inside the mouth and nose.

It can also affect areas around the eyes.

Hair can become white or gray in areas affected by vitiligo.

In some people, vitiligo can affect tissues related to the ear and may be associated with hearing changes.

If you notice new symptoms involving your eyes, ears, or other parts of your body, tell your doctor.

## Common Questions

**Is vitiligo contagious?**
No. You cannot catch vitiligo from another person.

**Is vitiligo caused by poor hygiene?**
No. Dirt and poor hygiene do not cause vitiligo.

**Is vitiligo dangerous?**
Vitiligo mainly affects skin color. It is usually not harmful to general health, but it can affect emotional wellbeing.

**Does vitiligo always spread?**
No. It can remain stable in some people, while other people develop new or larger patches.

**Does vitiligo hurt?**
Usually no. Most patches do not hurt, although mild itching can happen.

**Can food cure vitiligo?**
No special food or diet has been proven to cure vitiligo.

**Can stress cause vitiligo?**
Stress may be associated with changes in some people, but it is not the only cause.

**Can vitiligo be completely cured?**
There is currently no guaranteed complete cure, but treatment can help restore color or reduce the appearance of patches.

## When should I see a doctor?

You should talk to a doctor or dermatologist if you notice a new white or very light patch on your skin.

You should also seek medical advice if:

* Existing patches are changing
* New patches are appearing
* You are not sure whether you have vitiligo
* You want to start treatment
* The condition is affecting your confidence or emotional health
* You are worried about other symptoms

A dermatologist can examine your skin and decide whether you need further tests or treatment.

Do not diagnose yourself only from a picture because other skin conditions can also cause white or light patches.

## Important Safety Message

The information above is general health information about vitiligo.

It does not replace a medical examination or personal advice from a doctor.

Do not start prescription creams, steroid tablets, phototherapy, surgery, or depigmentation treatment without medical advice.

If you have new white patches, spreading patches, or symptoms that worry you, speak with a dermatologist.

Vitiligo can be difficult emotionally, but many people with vitiligo live normal and healthy lives.
